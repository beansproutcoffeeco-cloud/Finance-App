# Track B — Training Science Research Brief

## 1. Eccentric quad loading for downhill sports
Alpine ski research applies well to snowboarding: both involve sustained eccentric quad
work absorbing gravitational/turning forces, and eccentric quad/hamstring fatigue
persists 24+ hours after skiing and is a flagged injury risk (Bahr/Berg-type findings;
PubMed 27900115). Eccentric-emphasis training raises max strength, shifts peak tension
to longer muscle lengths, and improves eccentric coordination (Douglas/Roig-lineage
reviews; J Appl Physiol 2013). Tempo-squat work with a slow eccentric (3–5 s lowering)
drives quad hypertrophy and contractile change (Wilk et al.). **Programmable:** 3–4
sets × 6–8 reps back/goblet/split squats with 3–4 s eccentric, 1–2×/week; progress to
heel-elevated squats and eccentric-overload (Spanish squat, slow step-downs).
"Leg-blaster"-style ski-prep circuits run 3×/week in the final ~4 pre-season weeks
(Mountain Tactical Institute). *Contested:* eccentric-specific superiority over normal
tempo lifting for a novice is modest — technique and total load matter more early.

## 2. Isometric endurance for sustained knee-flexed postures
Snowboarding demands minutes of continuous quad isometric work; wall-sit endurance
benchmarks: ~90 s = good, ~120 s = excellent double-leg. Oranchuk et al. (2019, Scand
J Med Sci Sports) — the key systematic review — shows isometric strength is
**angle-specific** (train near ~90–120° knee flexion), long-muscle-length holds give
best hypertrophy/transfer, and high intensity (≥70% MVC) is needed for tendon
adaptation; explosive-intent holds build rapid force, sustained holds build endurance.
**Programmable:** wall sits and split-squat isometric holds starting 20–30 s × 3–4
sets, progressing ~10–15% duration/week toward 60–120 s holds (advanced target: 4 sets
× 2 min with 2 min rest), 2–3×/week. Mix one long fatigue-resistance hold with shorter
near-max holds. *Contested:* whether isometrics transfer to dynamic on-snow endurance
as well as dynamic tempo work — consensus is to pair both.

## 3. Safe plyometric progression (relative novice)
NSCA (Potach & Chu, CSCS Essentials Ch. 18) is the standard. Beginners: ≤5
low-to-moderate-intensity drills, **~80–100 foot contacts/session**, 2×/week with
48–72 h between sessions; intermediates ~100–120. Landing mechanics and low-amplitude
"extensive" plyos (pogo hops, low box step-offs, submaximal jumps) come first,
progressing to "intensive" work (depth jumps, bounding) only after competent landings
and a strength base (NSCA suggests ~1.5× bodyweight squat before high-intensity depth
jumps — a novice will not meet this, so stay extensive-to-moderate). **Tendon caveat:**
Bohm & Arampatzis (2015 meta-analysis, Sports Medicine Open) found plyometrics alone
did *not* reliably increase tendon stiffness — isometric/heavy-slow loading (≥70–90%
MVC, ≥3 s time-under-tension) drives tendon adaptation, and remodeling needs
**>12 weeks**. Plyos build reactive power; isometrics/heavy work build the tendon.

## 4. Lower-body power within an 18-week novice block
Consensus supports **strength-first, then power** sequencing for novices: a max-strength
base potentiates later power work. Contrast/complex training (heavy lift paired with a
matched plyo, exploiting post-activation potentiation) is well-supported *for trained
athletes* — Cormier et al. (2020 meta-analysis) and PAPE reviews show potentiation
scales with strength and training age; lifters with <1 yr experience show
little-to-no PAPE effect. **Implication:** spend the first ~8–10 weeks building
strength (3–5 sets × 5–8 reps, progressive load), then introduce simple power work
(jump squats, loaded jumps at 30–40% 1RM, 3–5 sets × 3–5 reps, full recovery), and only
late-block experiment with light contrast pairings. Periodization type matters little
for novices — nearly any structured progression works (Williams et al. 2017
meta-analysis).

## 5. Aerobic base without compromising strength
The interference effect is now viewed as largely avoidable at recreational volumes.
Schumann et al. (2022, ~43-study meta-analysis) found concurrent training did **not**
meaningfully blunt max strength (SMD −0.06, ns) or hypertrophy (SMD −0.01, ns) — but
**did** slightly reduce explosive-strength gains (SMD −0.28, p = 0.007), which matters
given the power goal. **Modality:** cycling interferes less than running with
lower-body strength/power (less eccentric muscle damage) — favor cycling. **Dose:**
Zone 2 for mitochondrial/aerobic base (fatigue resistance for repeated runs and
altitude), plus 1–2 HIIT doses/week (e.g. 4×4 min at ~90% HRmax, ≥2 min recovery) for
VO2max (Gibala; Milanović meta-analyses). Separate cardio from lifting by ≥6 h or put
strength first same-session to protect power. *Contested:* Zone 2 vs HIIT primacy for
VO2max — HIIT is more time-efficient; Zone 2 builds the durable base. Use both.

## Design implications — 18-week novice block (deload every 4th week)
- Weeks 1–4 (base/anatomical adaptation): technique-focused strength (3×8–10), landing
  drills + extensive plyos (60–80 contacts), Zone 2 work, short isometric holds
  (20–30 s). Deload wk 4.
- Weeks 5–8 (strength): heavier strength (4–5×5–6), tempo/eccentric squats (3–4 s
  down), holds to 45–60 s, plyos ~80–100 contacts 2×/week. Deload wk 8.
- Weeks 9–12 (strength→power+endurance): retain strength, add jump squats/loaded jumps
  (light, full recovery), holds to 60–90 s, contacts ~100–120, add a second weekly
  interval dose. Deload wk 12.
- Weeks 13–16 (power + sport-specific endurance): ski-prep eccentric circuits 2–3×/week,
  wall-sit/split-squat holds to 90–120 s, reactive plyos, optional light contrast
  pairings. Deload wk 16.
- Weeks 17–18 (peak/taper): cut volume ~40–50%, keep intensity, sharpen power and long
  holds, drop hard cardio to maintenance — arrive fresh.
- Cross-cutting: cardio and lifting ≥6 h apart or on different days; favor cycling;
  tendon adaptation needs the full 18 weeks, so keep heavy-slow/isometric loading
  present throughout; keep true contrast/PAPE work minimal until late; deloads = ~40–50%
  volume cut, intensity held, not full rest.
